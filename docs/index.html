AuthorThomas Miller

OKR Agents is a prototype for understanding an organisation's goals as a system rather than as a document. It takes the vocabulary most people already know, Objectives and Key Results, and turns a goal tree into something you can fly through, interrogate, and be briefed on. A coach answers questions about the whole tree by moving the view: it flies the camera to the nodes it means, pulses them, and rewrites them in place when asked. A briefing reads the tree unprompted, ranks what needs a person's attention, and hands each finding to the team that owns it. Each team carries a charter written as a job description, so a team is something you can open and talk to: it answers from its own mission, process and dependencies rather than as a generic assistant.

The prototype was built for a take-home exercise whose theme was Exploration & Understanding: complex systems and unfamiliar artifacts are hard to understand through static explanation, so build a tool that helps people develop deep understanding of one. The artifact chosen here is a goal hierarchy. A goal tree is a small complex system: dozens of nodes, weighted links, ownership that cuts across the hierarchy, and dependencies between teams that the tree itself cannot show. The bet in the interaction design is that the model's output is the interface, not a chat log next to one. Every answer is also a camera move, a highlight, or an edit you can undo.

The bundled default dataset is not a company. It models the sub-libraries of an open-source monorepo, illustratively Babylon.js, as teams with the dependencies they really have, plus an issue-triage team whose charter reads as the job you would hand to an agent. Its OKRs and every number in it are invented.

Why a goal tree, for this theme

OKRs are usually kept in a spreadsheet or a slide. Both are static explanations of a system that is not static: a key result supports its objective a little or a lot, a team owns some nodes and depends on other teams for others, and the places where the plan is quietly broken (an activity dressed up as an outcome, a key result parked under the wrong objective, a dependency nobody wrote down) are invisible until someone reads every row. Reading every row is the job that never gets done.

The prototype treats the tree the way one would treat any unfamiliar complex system: give it a shape you can look at, let you ask it questions and have the answers point, and have it volunteer what it thinks is wrong. Three ideas carry that:

  1. Pointing beats describing. Every reply from the model carries a list of node ids to highlight, and the camera flies to them. "Which key results don't support a company objective?" is answered by the view changing, with the prose as a caption. The same channel works in reverse: whatever you have selected, opened, or are typing into is sent to the model with each message, so "make this measurable" resolves without an id.
  2. The system should read itself. On load, and after every change, the tree is audited without being asked. The result is a ranked list of three to six findings, each with a one-click fix that goes through the same undoable commit path as a hand edit. This is the difference between a viewer and a tool for understanding: it tells you where to look.
  3. Ownership is a first-class object. Teams are not a string in an "owner" column. Each has a charter (mission, process, what it owns, whom it depends on) written so that a model can speak as that team. Two of the six findings on the default dataset come off the dependency lists alone, which is the first evidence that modelling the reliance graph pays for itself.

A tour of the prototype

The tree

The largest bundled dataset, a fictional 135-node conglomerate with 61 units. Green edges are strong links; orange and red edges are the weak ones the audit will find.

The scene is a three-level tree: one company node at the top, objectives below it, key results below those. Every node has a label, an owner, a metric, a target, and a contributes score from 0 to 1 that says how well it supports its parent. That score drives the edge: thick and green for a strong link, thin and red for a weak one, so misalignment reads at a glance from the whole-tree view.

The tree draws exactly one edge per node, the parent link, on purpose. Every relation that is not a parent edge (a key result that also serves a second objective, a team another team waits on) lives elsewhere: in the briefing, in the chat, and in future matrix views described under § Vision. That restraint is what keeps a 135-node scene legible.

Navigation is deliberately keyboard-first: F frames the whole tree, the arrow keys walk it (up to the parent, down to the first child, left and right between siblings), and every step selects the node and flies the camera to frame it with its parent and children. Programmatic camera moves always end level with world-up, so you never arrive at a node upside down; free tumbling with the mouse stays free. Hovering a node shows a card with its label, owner, metric and target.

Asking, and being shown

"Which key results don't clearly support a company objective?" The reply names them, and the ten nodes it means turn red in the scene. A "show 10 nodes" pill re-highlights them later.

The chat panel is where you ask. Every message goes to the service with the entire tree, the company structure, a short history, and a view context: which node is selected, which panel is open, which field the cursor is in, which nodes are currently highlighted. The model answers with one structured call containing three things: a reply, a list of node ids to highlight, and a list of actions to apply. Most messages are questions, so the actions list is usually empty; the highlight list almost never is, and it is what makes the tool feel alive.

Because actions are structured edits rather than text, "rewrite those so they're measurable" produces real changes to the tree, applied through the same commit path as a hand edit and undoable in one step. Chat has tabs, each with a mode and a subject. Beside the free-form coach there are interview tabs ("interview me about this OKR", "interview me about a new key result here") in which the assistant asks the questions and closes by proposing edits.

Being told what matters

The briefing on the 135-node tree: six findings, ranked, each naming the teams involved and offering Show, Fix, or Dismiss.

The briefing is the prototype's answer to "where should I look first?" When a document loads, and after every change to it, the assistant audits the whole tree without being asked and returns three to six ranked findings. Each is a card: a title, a sentence on why it matters, the teams it touches, and buttons. Show flies the camera to the nodes involved and pulses them. Fix applies the finding's proposed edit through the undoable commit path. Dismiss hides it. Where a finding resolves to a single chartered team, a fourth button, Ask <team>, opens a conversation with that team about it.

After Show on a finding: the camera has flown to the objective and the key result it names.

The list is a ranked window, not a queue: fix one problem and the next-ranked one takes its place, so the count holds at five or six on a tree with more problems than that. Findings are cached in the browser keyed by a hash of the document, so reloading, undoing to a tree already audited, or switching back to a dataset shows the earlier findings without another call. Only a document the browser has never seen costs an audit.

On the default dataset, six problems were planted deliberately (three weak links, two unmeasurable key results, one team with too many key results). The audit found those, grouped two of them into one finding about "activities, not outcomes", and then found two more nobody planted, both read straight off the teams' dependency lists: a team that says it depends on four others, only two of which have a key result that would deliver anything to it.

Teams that answer for themselves

The Issue triage team's charter: a mission, a process, what it owns, and the teams it depends on. "Talk to this team" is the front door onto the persona mode.

Teams are the structure behind the tree. The Teams panel lists them as a hierarchy with the number of key results each owns; opening one shows its charter. A charter is written as a job description for an agent: if a person read only the charter they should be able to sit in a meeting and argue that team's corner. Its four parts are the mission (why the team exists), the process (how it does its work), what it owns (the metrics and systems it is the authority on), and whom it depends on. The field descriptions and placeholder text read like a persona, not a form, because the same prose becomes a system prompt.

Talking to Issue triage. Asked which three of its six key results it would keep, it answers from its charter, disowns the CI key result as belonging to another team, and the seven nodes it refers to pulse in the tree.

Talk to this team opens a chat tab in the persona mode. The system prompt is the coach prompt plus the team's charter and an instruction to answer as that team would, from its priorities and constraints. Replies carry a speaker label, and, like every other reply, a highlight list, so the team's answer also moves the view. In the shot on the left the triage team, asked to cut its six key results to three, keeps the three that match its charter's promise, folds two into process, and says the sixth is not its job at all, which is the same conclusion the briefing reached from the other direction.

This is mode 1 of four discussion modes designed for the product (§ Vision). The other three, in which teams review each other's subtrees, debate with the user moderating, and negotiate edits, are additive to what exists: a tab already has a mode and a subject, and the service is stateless, so a multi-team debate is the browser orchestrating persona calls over a shared transcript.

Editing in place, with history

The detail panel in edit mode: label, owner (a team picker), metric, target, the parent it supports, and the fit score. Below, the five key results beneath it with their own fit.

Selecting a node opens its detail panel: the card, the parent it supports, its fit, and the nodes beneath it. Edit makes every field editable in place; each commits on blur or Enter. The owner is a team picker rather than free text, so ownership stays consistent with the company structure. Nodes and teams can be added, deleted, and re-parented from the same panel.

Every mutation, whether from a hand edit, a chat reply, a briefing fix, or an interview's closing proposal, is an Action applied through one commit() function. That function snapshots the document for undo and appends a described change to a history that rides along in export and import. It is the one invariant the whole design leans on: there is no UI path that changes the tree without going through it, which is what makes "undo the whole negotiation in one step" possible later.

Datasets

The default dataset's fifteen units: a project's sub-libraries as teams, each with a charter.

The app boots on a bundled dataset and ships five, switchable from the top bar. Each is a hand-written document that passes the schema with no warnings and meets a demo bar of at least three weak links and two unmeasurable key results, so the briefing always has something to say.

Time spent, and how it was built

Honest accounting

The brief asked for one to two hours on the prototype, with an allowance of up to eight. I spent about four hours of my own attention on it, spread over 11–14 September 2026, and will spend about one more hour recording the video. Claude Code ran for much longer than that: the work went through 32 recorded sessions (25 top-level, 7 subagents) and 44 commits, most of them while I was doing something else. Every session is published unedited under § Transcripts so the split between my time and the model's is inspectable rather than asserted.

The build followed one loop, repeated seventeen times: write a plan, implement the plan, write completion notes. The repository's working instructions require every non-trivial change to start as a plan file with a fixed template (context, goal, decisions locked in, numbered phases citing file:line edit points, verification, out of scope) and to end with three bullets of completion notes: what landed as planned, what changed mid-flight, and what surprised. Design rationale lives apart from the plans in concept documents, so that the why is not fused with the what. The plans are listed with their transcripts in § Plans implemented.

The rough sequence, by day:

Day 1 (11 Sept)
A README written as a set of build notes (kept unedited at the bottom of the repository's README), then the first working app from it. The same day: a plan for a shared, versioned JSON Schema package, and its implementation; then a round of six plans written together for datasets, camera behaviour, the node editor, the team model, view context and chat tabs. Three of those were implemented in parallel by subagents in isolated worktrees, the rest in sequence.
Day 2 (12 Sept)
The hover card, keyboard shortcuts, neighbourhood framing, a large dataset for scale, several small bugs, and then the two things that changed the product: the vision and concept documents (written when 80% of the time budget was spent, to decide what the remaining time was for), and the tree briefing with the persona mode as its stretch goal.
Day 3–4 (13–14 Sept)
Container images, CI and a CORS gate for deployment; the audit cache; the Babylon.js dataset; and finally the rename from "OKR Viewer" to "OKR Agents" with a front door on the persona mode, which is the point at which the product's name caught up with its thesis.

Two habits from that process are worth naming because they are visible in the transcripts. First, completion notes are where the real engineering knowledge ended up: the discovery that naming a field the tool schema does not have makes the model emit an empty tool call, or that a nginx proxy_pass with a trailing slash silently breaks the API while the health check stays green, are both recorded in the plan that found them, not in a commit message. Second, the plans were written before the code they cite existed in its final form, so several completion notes open with "every file:line citation had drifted", and the fix was to re-verify each before editing rather than to trust the plan.

Design decisions

The decisions below are the ones that shaped everything else. Each is recorded, with its rationale, in the plan or concept document that made it.

The model's output is the interface
Every reply is a structured object with a highlight list and an action list, and the scene reacts to both. The prose is secondary. This is why the tree is a 3D scene and not a table: a table cannot be flown through, and a highlight in a table is a row colour.
One tool, forced
Rather than four tools (edit_node, add_node, relink_node, highlight_nodes) and a multi-round tool loop, the service defines a single respond tool and forces it with tool_choice. One call, guaranteed structure, no branch for "what if it called nothing". The tool's schema is generated from the same JSON Schemas the app validates against, and it varies by mode: the audit mode's schema has a findings field and no other mode's does, because a mode whose schema cannot express findings must not be told about them.
The service is stateless; the browser owns the document
The whole tree is sent with every message. There is no session store and no drift between what the service thinks the tree is and what is on screen. It also means a multi-team debate can be orchestrated from the browser as a sequence of persona calls over a shared transcript.
Every mutation is an Action with an actor, through one commit()
Hand edits, chat edits, briefing fixes and interview proposals all take the same path, so undo, history, export and re-audit come for free with every new feature. A future executor agent adds actor: "agent" and nothing else changes.
The schema is the contract, owned in one place
A workspace package holds nine draft-07 JSON Schemas (node, tree, company, history, action, chat request and response, document envelope) and builds TypeScript types, precompiled validators, and the Claude tool schema from them. Both the web app and the service import it. Every field description is written for the model as much as for a person.
Teams carry charters written for a prompt
The charter is the team's persona. Field descriptions say "in the team's own words". This is what lets "Talk to this team" be a one-plan feature and what the later discussion modes build on.
Audit on change, not on schedule
The briefing re-runs after every commit, debounced. A cache keyed by a hash of the document keeps that from costing an API call for any tree the browser has seen before.
The three-level ladder is a default, not doctrine
The schema only warns about depth; the editor and the prompt enforce it. Deeper hierarchies can be opened later without a migration. The argument for why this matters is in Depth and Focus.

Architecture

┌──────────────────────┐         ┌─────────────────────┐         ┌────────┐
│  web/  (Vite+Svelte) │  POST   │  service/ (Express) │  HTTPS  │ Claude │

Three packages in one npm workspace:

  • schema/ (about 3,700 lines including tests): the JSON Schemas, a build script that emits types, validators and the tool schema, semantic checks (ownership, level ladder, dangling references), and a versioned document envelope with a migrations directory. 129 tests.
  • web/ (about 3,700 lines of Svelte and JS): App.svelte owns the document state, the undo stack, history and the audit trigger; Graph.svelte wraps 3d-force-graph and owns the camera; Chat.svelte, Briefing.svelte, Detail.svelte, TeamList.svelte and TeamEditor.svelte are the panels; lib/ holds pure functions for applying actions, walking the tree, canonicalising a document for hashing, and the IndexedDB audit cache. Two of those have their own tests.
  • service/ (about 900 lines): one endpoint, POST /api/chat. prompt.js holds the system prompt, the per-mode role blocks and the respondTool(mode) builder; validate.js sanitises everything that references the document (context, history, tab subject, actions, highlights, findings) against this tree, dropping what it cannot resolve rather than failing the request. A CORS gate refuses browser origins other than the app's.

The request carries the tree, the company structure, the message, the selected node, a history slice, the tab's mode and subject, and the view context. The response is { reply, actions, highlight }, plus findings in audit mode. Both are validated against the schema on both sides.

For deployment, each package has a Dockerfile built from the repository root (one lockfile serves all three). The web image is nginx over a vite build, proxying /api/ to the service so the browser talks to one origin and the API key never reaches a bundle. A GitHub Actions workflow builds both images on every push, publishes them from main, and smoke-tests the pair with docker compose. The live demo runs on Cloud Run.

Vision and future plans

The mission, as written on the README's first screen: OKR Agents uses the OKR vocabulary people already know to make traditional OKRs lighter to run, and to manage teams of agents through the same lens. The prototype is Horizon 1 of three. Each later horizon has a concept document that fixes its vocabulary and the seams the current code keeps open for it; none has a plan yet, and writing the plan is the first step.

HorizonWhatWhy it waits
1. Interaction model and value (built)The tree, the coach, the briefing, teams with charters, the persona mode.Everything later assumes the tree, teams and chat are worth using by one person.
2. Agents that work and measureAn agent runs as a team to execute one of its key results through connected tools, records what it did and how the metric moved, and a progress dashboard reads the record.Needs a real charter (done), a place to run long jobs, and tool connections with credentials.
3. Shared, live documentThe document lives in a realtime store so several people and agents edit and chat against it at once.Needs the interaction model settled first; a multi-user store hardens whatever shape it is given.

Team agent discussions (nearest to hand)

Four discussion modes were chosen, in dependency order. Mode 1 is shipped; the rest are additive to the chat tab's mode enum and the stateless service.

  1. Chat with one team's agent. Shipped as the persona mode behind "Talk to this team".
  2. Agents review each other's OKRs. Team A's persona critiques Team B's subtree for alignment, measurability and unstated dependencies on A. One call per review; the output is a normal reply, highlight and optional actions.
  3. A team-versus-team debate the user moderates. Several personas over one shared transcript; the browser orchestrates, one persona call per turn, with the user able to interject and the transcript shown with speaker labels.
  4. Agents negotiate and propose edits. Mode 3 with a closing turn: a neutral coach call reads the transcript and returns actions, which go through commit(), so the whole negotiation is one undo step.

Concept: Teams as Agents - Design.

Agents that execute and measure

The example that defines the feature is already in the default dataset. The Issue triage team has the key result "address 90% of incoming issues, each ranked by priority". Its executor, running with the team's charter as its system prompt, would connect to the repository through a connection the user has authorised, triage and label incoming issues on a schedule, record how it is doing against the key result as metric samples, and leave a trail the user can inspect, undo where the tool allows, and turn off. The schema already has a place for those samples (history.metricSamples), and a person can record one by hand for any key result today; the dashboard reads the same shape whoever wrote it. The concept fixes the vocabulary (executor, connection, run, metric sample, progress) and the invariant that an agent which can report hourly must not.

Concept: Agents as Executors - Design.

Matrix views

Three tables that show what the tree cannot, because the tree draws only parent edges. OKR fitness: rows and columns are both the OKRs, and each cell scores how well the row fits the column on a signed scale, so "in tension with" is expressible. Team reliance: rows and columns are teams, each cell how much the row relies on the column, derived from the charters and the tree as well as asserted, so the disagreement between what a team says it depends on and what its key results actually need is the finding. Team communication: the same axes, counting how often two teams actually talk. Reliance is what teams say; communication is what they do; the two overlaid is the payoff. Four plans in dependency order when they are written: reliance matrix (its source data already exists), fitness matrix, team message board, comms connectors.

Concept: Matrix Views - Design.

Deeper hierarchies, and the focus budget

The received wisdom that OKR cascades should stop at three levels was protecting against alignment fatigue in a manual system. With a model carrying the alignment work, the bottleneck moves from administrative capacity to human focus: a model can draft a thousand perfectly aligned key results and a person can still hold three to five priorities. So the product's job shifts from drawing the tree to keeping it honest and small: a live alignment audit on every change (the briefing is the seed) and a per-team focus budget the assistant pushes back with ("you now have nine key results; which three matter this quarter?"). Depth in the data model stays open; depth in the editor waits for those two.

Concept: Depth and Focus - Design. Overall: OKR Agents - Product Vision.

Known limitations

  • Nothing persists. The document lives in the browser; reload and you are back to the bundled dataset (the audit cache survives, the edits do not). Export and Import are the workaround. Horizon 3 is the fix.
  • The audit is slow on big trees. Fifteen to twenty seconds on the 135-node dataset, eight to fifteen on the small ones. The panel never blocks and old cards stay up under "Reviewing…", but a demo should load the document before the audience is looking.
  • Cost is one audit per commit, debounced at 1.5 seconds and cached by document hash. It is the obvious thing to gate if the tool were used at length.
  • "Ask <team>" is rare by design. Findings are grouped by problem and problems cross teams, so about one finding in six on the bundled datasets resolves to a single team.
  • The one-tool design has one failure mode. If the model's structured call comes back malformed, the service falls back to "I couldn't come up with an answer for that". A retry-once would be a cheap follow-up.
  • Single user, no auth, no streaming, desktop only. The README's scope boundary from day one, unchanged.
  • The datasets are invented. The Babylon.js package layout is real; its OKRs, numbers and every finding about them are not statements about the real project.
